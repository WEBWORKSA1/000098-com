Color psychology in marketing: what the evidence actually says

By the 000098 editorial team · Updated October 14, 2026 · 7 min read

Contents
  1. What is reasonably well supported
  2. Claims to treat with caution
  3. Common associations (useful defaults)
  4. How to test colors properly
  5. The takeaway

Color psychology is full of confident charts: blue means trust, red means hunger, green means money. Some of it holds up, much of it is overstated. Here is a sober view you can actually use for branding and conversion decisions.

What is reasonably well supported

  • Color aids recognition. Consistent use of a distinctive color makes a brand easier to spot on a shelf or in a feed. The effect comes from consistency and distinctiveness, not from the specific hue.
  • Fit matters more than the color itself. Studies on brand personality find people judge whether a color seems appropriate for the product. A playful pink suits a candy brand better than a law firm.
  • Contrast drives attention. An element that differs from its surroundings gets noticed. A "red button beats green" test usually measures contrast with the page, not redness.
  • Cultural meaning is real. White is associated with weddings in much of the West and with mourning in parts of East Asia; red is lucky in China; saffron carries religious significance in India.

Claims to treat with caution

  • That a single color produces a specific emotion in everyone. Personal experience and culture override generic associations.
  • Precise statistics such as "90% of snap judgments are based on color" that circulate without a traceable study.
  • That changing a button color will lift conversions by a fixed percentage on your site. Results do not transfer between pages.

Common associations (useful defaults)

With those caveats, broad associations can guide a first draft. Deep blues such as #000098 read as stable, serious and competent, which is why finance, technology and healthcare use them. Greens suggest growth and health. Reds and oranges suggest energy and urgency. Black suggests premium and modern. Pastels suggest softness and approachability.

How to test colors properly

  1. Change one thing at a time and keep the copy constant.
  2. Run the test long enough to reach statistical significance, usually thousands of visitors per variant for typical conversion rates.
  3. Measure the outcome you care about (sign-ups, revenue), not clicks alone.
  4. Check accessibility; a variant that wins but fails contrast is a liability.

The takeaway

Choose colors that fit your audience and category, make them distinctive versus competitors, use them consistently, and give your calls to action strong contrast. That combination beats any color-meaning chart.
